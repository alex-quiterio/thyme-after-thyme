# How to improvise a vegetable meal

The most useful recipe in this book isn't written in quantities. It's this one:

## Start with heat

Put a frying pan on the heat.

Add olive oil.

Give the vegetables enough heat and space to brown. If everything is crammed into a small pan, it will steam.

## Build an aromatic base

Onion + garlic is the obvious start.

Ginger + garlic is another.

Leek + thyme.

Chilli + garlic.

Cumin + onion.

There is no sacred combination.

## Season in layers

A little salt at the start.

More later.

Acid at the end.

This is usually better than throwing everything in at once.

## Create contrast

Ask what the dish is missing.

Too soft? Add toasted seeds.

Too rich? Add lemon.

Too bland? Add salt.

Too salty? Add volume without salt: water or acid.

Too sweet? Add vinegar.

Too dry? Add sauce.

Too wet? Turn up the heat and give it space.

## Finish with intention

Fresh herbs.

Lemon zest.

Toasted nuts.

Olive oil.

Chilli flakes.

Sesame seeds.

A spoonful of yoghurt.

They aren't decoration. They are part of the architecture.

## A final table

Vegetarian cooking is sometimes described as a problem of substitution: what replaces the meat? That is the wrong question.

The more interesting question is: what happens when the vegetable becomes the protagonist?

A mushroom doesn't need to pretend to be a steak. A leek doesn't need to become something else. A potato can be the whole emotional centre of dinner, if it's well roasted.

The trick isn't to make vegetables behave like meat. The trick is to understand what vegetables can do that meat can't: their bitterness, sweetness, water, perfume, colour, tenderness, crunch, fermentation and freshness.

Cook them over high heat. Cook them slowly. Leave some raw. Burn some. Purée some. Pickle some. Put them side by side and let them argue.

A good meal isn't a solved equation. It's a small ecosystem. And like any ecosystem, it becomes interesting through its relationships.

Eat well.
